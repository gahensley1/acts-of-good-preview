Who the panel is missing

Six to add, and what each one is for. Every reason below is checked against real numbers, not my impression of who ought to be there.

First, plainly

I can’t go out and recruit real testers — nobody on this panel is a person, including the seven you have. They are written characters who review in character, and they are only ever as good as what they are built out of.

So what I went and got instead was the real data on who actually does this — who volunteers, who helps informally, at what age — and built the new ones out of that. It is the difference between inventing seven more opinions and filling the holes the current seven leave.

Your seven run from Brianne at 30 to Paula at 52. That is a twenty-two year band in the middle of life, and everything outside it is untested.

Which would matter less if the outside were quiet. It isn’t. Baby Boomers do more informal helping than any other generation — 58.7%, against about 28% of the whole population doing formal volunteering. And informal helping — the bins, the lift, the casserole, the sitting-with — is exactly what this app was built to record. The people most likely to fill a year of it are the ones nobody on your panel is.

The six

1
Eileen Prosser, 68
Retired, widowed two years. Does more for people than anyone you have tested.

Drives three people to appointments. Cooks for a neighbour with MS. Runs the flowers rota at church. Has never called any of it volunteering and would be embarrassed to. Uses an iPhone her son set up, and has never knowingly installed anything.

She is the single most likely real user of this app and the least likely to get through setup. She will find every place it assumes you know what a PWA is, what “Add to Home Screen” means, or where a downloaded file goes.

Why her: Boomers lead informal helping at 58.7%, and women’s volunteering peaks at 65+. Your panel stops at 52.
2
Kayla Ocampo, 19
Sophomore, works retail, posts constantly, gives away hours she does not have.

Tutors two kids a week. Organises the campus food-pantry drive on a group chat. Her whole social life is on a phone and she has an unforgiving eye for anything that looks like an adult trying to sound young.

She is the one who tells you in five seconds whether the card is postable, whether the hashtag is mortifying, and whether the gold script reads as lovely or as a greetings-card aisle.

Why her: Gen Z volunteers at 24.5% and nothing in the app’s voice, card or hashtag is aimed at anyone under 30.
3
Desmond Iyer, 37
Blind since 19. Screen-reader user. Reviews nothing but whether the app can be used at all.

Not a sighted person imagining it. He goes through every screen by ear and by keyboard and reports what he actually reaches, what he cannot, and what the app reads out to him that isn’t what is on the screen.

He would have caught this morning’s bug on day one — a box labelled Anything you want to remember? that spoke aloud as How did it go?

Why him: Paula already flagged that the You screen “can’t be used without a touchscreen and good eyes”, and it has been sitting open ever since. It deserves someone whose whole job it is.
4
Ruth Kinnaird, 55
The methodical one. Reads every word in the app and checks whether it is true.

You asked for someone who digs into the content. This is her. She takes the 89 ideas one at a time and audits the line underneath each — the fact that is supposed to remove the excuse — asking whether it is actually true, still true, and true everywhere rather than in one city.

She is also the one who finds the same thing said three different ways on three screens, before seven testers have to.

Her method: every claim gets checked, sourced or struck. She reports in a table, not in feelings.
5
Priya Raghunathan, 43
The social one. Studies what happens after the post goes up.

The other one you asked for. Not a marketer — she thinks about what posting your own kindness does: to the friendship, to the person you helped, to the people reading it who cannot afford to do the same.

Trent gestures at this from inside his own discomfort. She can say what is actually happening, including the parts that work: why a named reason travels further than a number, and why an anonymous act still needs a witness.

Her beat: the card on a real feed, the person who was helped seeing it, and what fifty of these in a row does to a friendship.
6
Hollis Wray
The calendar expert. Owns the occasions engine and its lead times.

Your explicit ask, and a real specialism. He knows which dates are fixed, which float on a rule, and — the valuable part — the lead time before each one, which is the thing the app already understands better than most.

Not a tester. He audits and extends what is already built: 8 floating dates, 19 fixed occasions, 4 quiet-month entries.

His job: verify every date and rule; add what is missing; keep the lead times honest — background checks take weeks, wreath sponsorships close in November, Thanksgiving shifts fill in October.
