Build a winter shelter for the cats who live outside

This weekend · An hour · Under $20 confirmed

A thirty-gallon plastic tote with a twenty-gallon tote nested inside, a styrofoam slab on the floor, and straw. Never hay, which holds moisture and goes mouldy, and never blankets.

why: the cats are already there, and the shelter is the difference between a hard winter and a fatal one

Full build spec, and the reason for every material, on one page.  ·  Alley Cat Allies

Make one window safe for birds

What is short, and who needs it

This is the calendar seat’s second deliverable, and it may be worth more than its ideas. The app already has a card that says what is worth doing about now; this is the best version of the data behind it that exists. The one-line summary: December has more volunteers than it can use. January, February, July and August have the work and nobody to do it.

January

The emptiest month across the whole sector. December giving has been banked and spent, rotas collapse after the holidays, blood supply drops with travel and winter illness, and the August classroom supplies have run out.

MLK Day of Service — third Monday. National Mentoring Month. National Blood Donor Month. Free-tax-prep signup closes.

February

The second quiet month, and the cold one. Shelters need bedding. Teacher morale is at the bottom of the November–January slump. Cat adoptions are at their annual floor before kitten season.

Great Backyard Bird Count — the four days ending on the third Monday. Black History Month. February 14 is the hardest fixed date of the year for the recently widowed.

March

Tax season peaks and free sites are short of greeters and interpreters, not only preparers. Spring cleanup starts too early for overwintering insects.

Read Across America — March 2. National School Breakfast Week — first full week.

April

Kitten season begins and neonate intakes rise first. Shelters need bottle feeders, laundry and towels. Schools hit testing season.

Public Safety Telecommunicators Week — second full week. National Volunteer Week — the week beginning the third Sunday. Earth Day — April 22.

May

Kitten intake peaks sharply. Eastern Pacific hurricane season opens May 15 while supplies are still cheap and stocked.

Stamp Out Hunger — second Saturday. Teacher Appreciation Week — first full week. Nurses Week — May 6 to 12. Skilled Nursing Care Week — begins on Mother’s Day. Older Americans Month. Foster Care Month.

June

The summer gap opens. School meals stop with the last day of school, corporate and school drives end, Meals on Wheels routes lose drivers to vacations.

Atlantic hurricane season opens June 1. Summer meal sites open. National Trails Day — first Saturday. June 30 is the fiscal year end for about a fifth of US charities.

July

The hardest month for food banks alongside January. Blood supply falls because schools and colleges run most drives and they are closed. Heat kills more Americans than any other weather.

July 5 is the highest stray-dog intake day of the year. Act before the fourth, not on it.

August

Summer drought continues, then flips at the end of the month as back-to-school begins. Poll worker recruitment is happening now for November.

National Night Out — first Tuesday. Check the Chip Day — August 15. Poll worker recruitment — mid-August.

September

The second kitten intake peak, the one nobody plans for because everyone thinks kitten season ended in June. Hurricane risk peaks on the 10th.

September 11 Day of Service. Hunger Action Month. Preparedness Month. Coastal Cleanup — third Saturday. Public Lands Day — fourth Saturday. Voter Registration Day — fourth Tuesday.

October

The recruiting month. Cold-weather shelters train now for a season starting in November. Medicare’s window opens and closes inside eleven weeks. Leaf litter is being destroyed.

Medicare open enrollment — October 15 to December 7. Free-tax-prep signup opens. School Bus Safety Week — third full week. First Responders Day — October 28.

November

Everyone arrives. Thanksgiving shifts are oversubscribed by mid-month. New teachers enter the disillusionment phase. Family caregivers head into the worst six weeks of their year.

Veterans Day — November 11. Giving Tuesday — the Tuesday after Thanksgiving. Family Caregivers Month. Hospice and Palliative Care Month.

December

The most crowded month of the year for both giving and volunteering. Blood and platelets go short because donors travel. Care-home residents have a busy fortnight and then nothing.

Medicare open enrollment closes December 7. The solstice, around the 21st, is the anchor for longest-night services.

Never recommend with the evidence

Acts that feel good and do harm

The economics seat’s list, and it protects the product as much as the ideas grow it. Several of these are the most common entries in every kindness library in existence.

A canned food drive, as the default

A can bought at retail and then sorted, dated and shelved by staff is the most expensive way to put food in a pantry. Cash buys at wholesale and buys what is missing. The exception matters: buying off a published top-needs list is a specific request, not a guess — which is why our existing idea survives.

Sending goods to a disaster nobody asked for

The emergency management literature has a name for it: the second disaster. Trucks that should carry water carry junk, and somebody pays to landfill it. Cash and gift cards, always.

Orphanage volunteering, and voluntourism generally

The Lancet called it misguided altruism. Paying to spend two weeks with institutionalised children creates demand that keeps children in institutions. UNICEF, ECPAT and Better Care Network are unanimous. Never, in any form.

Turning up at a soup kitchen on Thanksgiving without booking

Some kitchens fill those shifts by August. Supervising a stranger for three hours costs real staff time. The act is asking which month they cannot staff.

One-off unscoped pro bono

A day of unsolicited expertise that leaves a strategy document nobody asked for is a cost. The test: did they define the deliverable, or did you? Our existing “day of professional skill” should carry that question in its fact.

Bags of used clothing left at a charity’s door

Most of it is not sellable and disposing of it is a cost centre. A specific closet asking for specific sizes in specific condition is the right shape — which is what our clothing-closet idea already is.

Donating a car through a for-profit intermediary

The middleman’s cut can leave the charity with a fraction of the sale price. Sell it and give the cash.

Donating credit card points or airline miles

Points redeemed to charity usually convert at a worse rate than cash back. Take the cash back and give the cash.

Judging a charity by its overhead ratio

Charity Navigator, GuideStar and the BBB jointly published a letter telling donors to stop. Over a third of nonprofits report zero fundraising costs, so the ratio measures accounting, not effectiveness.

Micro-restricting a small gift

“This $25 is for pencils only” obliges somebody to track $25 against a pencil budget. Below a few hundred dollars, restriction costs more than it directs.

Giving blood the day after a mass-casualty event

The blood used that day was donated weeks earlier. Post-tragedy surges expire unused and the shelf is empty two months later. Our blood ideas should always point at scheduling, never at reacting.

Starting a new drive, fund or nonprofit for something that already exists

The most expensive act of good available, and the one that feels best.

Rejected from the repositories so nobody rediscovers them next year

What the kindness libraries are mostly made of

The repository seat read the big idea banks and calendars and reported that they are built for volume, not for action. These are the entries that dominate them, each failing on a stated ground.

  • Smile at a stranger · spread positivity · give a compliment — slogans, not actions, and the single most common entry in every kindness calendar
  • Sticky notes on public bathroom mirrors — performative, and litter somebody else cleans
  • Tape quarters to the vending machine · pay for the car behind you — performative; the chain-of-kindness framing is about being seen
  • Hand a stranger a $5 gift card — money with no idea whether it helps, and it puts the receiver on the spot
  • Let someone go ahead of you in line — manners, not an act
  • Donate the books you have already read · used stuffed animals — most are binned; infection control requires new
  • A child’s free lemonade stand — an adult’s job dressed as a child’s act
  • Sponsor a child for $X a month via a platform — money handed to a platform rather than doing a thing
  • Sign this petition · post this to your story — performative, often party-political, nothing done
  • Adopt a highway — a formal agreement, a safety briefing, a multi-year commitment and a minimum group size
  • Crisis text counselling — thirty-plus hours of training and real risk of harm if done badly
  • Be My Eyes — eleven million volunteers against 1.1 million blind users. We would be selling an act that mostly does not happen

Three corrections to what we already have the economics seat, asked to be blunt

“Check your employer’s matching portal first, it can double you for free.”

Substantially right, and “can” saves it — real programmes run from half to three times and most cap annually. But the availability figure everyone quotes is Fortune 500 only; among small and mid-size employers it is closer to 28 percent. The bigger fact is not in the library at all: only about 1.3 percent of eligible donations are ever matched.

“Call the district food services office, not the school. Ask for the negative-balance total.”

Operationally excellent and worth keeping. Three caveats: some districts will not disclose or will not accept third-party payment; in states with universal free meals there may be no debt at all; and the act treats a symptom. A meaningful share of families carrying lunch debt qualify for free meals and never filed the form. The higher-leverage version — helping one family file — is missing from the library entirely.

“Buy the food bank’s actual top-needs list.”

Correct and safe, because it is a published request rather than a guess. But nothing tells a reader that cash still beats retail purchase even off the list. The top-needs list is right for people who want to shop; it is not the efficient frontier. A why line could say so warmly.
