50 Acts of Good · the sign-up sheet · item 18, continued

How do they find it again?

You asked the question that breaks what I built an hour ago. You were right. Here is what it was, what I have already done, and the part that is still yours.

Your first question

A real hole — my miss

“How do they get back to the list without having the app?”

They couldn’t. The way out only works if you can get back to the page, and nothing took them there.

No account, no app, no bookmark. Once they close the page the only routes back are walking past the poster again — which might be a church hallway they visit once a year — or still having the tab open a week later. So the give-back was a button almost nobody could reach.

It is exactly the kind of thing that looks finished in a test and isn’t finished in a life. I should have caught it; you did.

What I’ve already changed

done · built and tested

The calendar reminder is now the door back.

You had already ruled this and I hadn’t used it: your app shows what is outstanding, their calendar reminds them. The reminder is the one thing of ours that lands on their phone and stays there — so it now carries the link.

Nothing is sent, nothing is stored, and it cost one line.

You can free a row yourself.

Because most people who can’t come won’t open the page at all — they will text you, because that is what anybody does. You had their number; you had no way to put the pans back on the list.

Now the phone that made the sheet can open a row without anybody’s permission. Which is right: it is your sheet, and somebody just told you they can’t make Saturday.

Your second question

Working as intended — but worth explaining

“And also doesn’t it have a name and number requirement when you take it? We built that.”

We did, and it is still there. It just can’t be the thing that proves the row is yours.

The name and number go to you, so you can message someone about where and when. That is the whole job they do.

The moment they also become the way back in, two things go wrong:

A number is not a secret

Anyone on that street who knows Dana’s number could open her row — or take a thing off the list in her name. The poster is on a public wall.

It becomes a login

“Enter your phone number to continue” is the exact thing this page was built to avoid. And it still would not help — they need the link before they can type anything into it.

So the proof stays invisible: when they take a row, their own phone quietly keeps a key. Nothing to remember, nothing to type, nothing on the page that anyone else can see.

The honest trade: a new phone, or clearing the browser, loses the way out. That is the price of never asking anyone to sign up for anything — and it is covered, because you can free the row yourself when they text you.

Your third question — and you spotted the shape of it exactly

Yes — already working, and I checked rather than assumed

“Can the person scanning the QR code scan it again to gain access and be recognised? That seems like that will help one side.”

They can. Scanning the poster a second time is the way back, and it costs nothing because it is already built.

The code points at the page; the key sits in that browser against that address. So scanning the same poster a week later opens the same page and it knows the row is theirs — no login, no typing, nothing to remember. I ran it four ways on a phone-sized screen:

Yours, way out there Dana closes the page entirely, then scans the poster again a week later
Yours, way out there Same, with junk on the end of the address, as some scanners add
In good hands · nothing to press A different person’s phone, scanning the same poster
In good hands · nothing to press Her own phone, but opened in a different browser

The bottom two are the privacy you wanted, working: the row is only ever hers on the thing she scanned with. Nobody else can see or touch it, and the page still says nothing about who anyone is.

And here is the one side, which is what you were getting at

It completely solves the neighbours. It does nothing for the hallway.

Where it wins outright

Act 22 is exactly this shape. Twenty-eight flyers on twenty-eight front doors. Those people walk past the poster every single day. For them the paper is the way back, and there is nothing to build, explain or remember.

Where it does nothing

The church noticeboard you pass on a Sunday. The staff room you have left. The shop window you walked by once. They scanned it somewhere they are not going back to, and for them the poster might as well not exist.

So it is free, strong, and half the audience — which is why it sits alongside the calendar and your message rather than replacing them. The only thing actually missing is that nobody is told it works.

What’s left for you

18·1

The calendar, and nothing more

What is built now. Clean, quiet, uses a thing you had already decided.

The catch: it only reaches people who actually tap Put it in my calendar. Plenty won’t.

18·2

Show them the link as well  my recommendation

After they take something, the page shows the address in plain text under the calendar button — small, quiet, copyable. People screenshot it, or text it to themselves. That is what they already do with everything else.

Costs one line on the page and reaches everybody, not just the calendar-keepers.

18·3

Your message carries it  my recommendation

When the app writes the message telling them where and when, the link rides along in it. Then every single person who took a row has it in a text from someone they know, which is where they will actually look.

This one is in the app, and the app side of the sheet isn’t built yet — so it is a note on the spec rather than work today. It is the strongest of the three.

18·4

Tell them the poster works  my recommendation

One line on the page after they take something. Costs nothing, and it is the only part of this that is already built and simply unsaid.

It also quietly stops the sentence apologising for itself. The current line leads on what is missing; the new one leads on what works.
