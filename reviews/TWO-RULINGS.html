Two rulings, drawn

Everything else from the review round is built. These two are yours, because they are how the screen looks, not whether it works. Every picture below is really rendered by the app, at phone size, from the same eleven-act year.

Question one

The end of the year

The engineer who read the judgment rather than the code: “A visible ending is a real asset that almost nobody else has. But an empty dashed numbered square is the app’s own visual language for undone work, promoted to the middle of the screen for twelve months. Draw a horizon — a date, a destination — never an empty box you have not ticked.”

A
Leave it. The numbered square stays.
As it is now

The shape of the whole year from the first morning, and the fifty is a real number you are working toward. The objection: that dashed square is exactly what an unplanned act looks like in the grid below, so the eye reads it as one more thing undone.

B
A horizon. No box at all — the month you finish.
Drawn

The rule runs out to a destination instead of to a container. It is a date, not a task, so there is nothing to feel behind on. The month is worked out from the day you declared it. Costs the row nothing and gives the year an actual horizon.

C
The balloon, drawn faintly, where the box was.
Drawn

Jessica’s mark at the far end, waiting to be filled in — the same mark act zero already wears, so the row reads as one thing becoming another. The risk: a faint version of a mark that means “this happened” may read as a mark you have half earned.

D
A milepost. A coral tick, and the month under it.
Drawn

The quietest of the four. Keeps the row’s left-to-right travel and marks where it lands, without borrowing either the grid’s square or the card’s balloon. It is the least like anything else on the screen, which is either the point or the problem.

Question two

Where the journal sits

The same seat: the journal is the thing somebody comes back to read, and it lives below ten rows of tiles, where nobody scrolling for thirty seconds will ever meet it. Its recommendation was to move it above the grid.

A · Leave it below the grid The first screen is the year: what you have done, what is worth doing, and the tiles starting. Reading the journal is a deliberate trip you take when you want it.

B · Move it above the grid The journal is now impossible to miss — and it has pushed The 50 to the last line of the screen. On a phone this size the grid no longer appears at all until you scroll.

The argument for moving it, at full height. In A the journal is the second-to-last thing on a screen five and a half phones tall. In B it is the fourth thing you see. The cost is equally plain: it puts a door to another screen in front of the year itself.

There is a third answer nobody asked for, and I will draw it if you want it: leave the button where it is and put the journal’s own count on it — “Read your year · 11 pages” — so it earns attention where it stands.

Answer however is easiest — 1B 2A, or in words. If nothing here is right, say what is wrong with it and I will draw again. Neither of these is built; the whole of the rest of 5O is.
