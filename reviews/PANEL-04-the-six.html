The six, run separately

None of them saw the app at the same time as another. None saw another’s notes. None knew who else was looking. That is the whole point, and it is why the places they agree are worth something.

Fix this one first

Wrong, checked, and it puts somebody in the wrong place

The app sends people to Wreaths Across America a week early. Every year.

It is set to the second Saturday of December. The real day is the Saturday between the 13th and the 19th — it has been, every year from 2016 to 2030.

What the app says
12 Dec 2026
The actual day
19 Dec 2026

Somebody stands in a cold cemetery seven days before anyone else arrives. And the advice under it says sponsorships close in November — but the entry does not appear until December, after its own deadline has gone.

It is one line.

Eileen Prosser, 68
Does more for people than anyone on the panel. Would be locked out in a week.
Would not keep using it

Stopped on the first screen — four numbers before she had done anything, two of them the same thing twice. Then the backup page: “Choose Save to Files and put it in iCloud Drive”, sitting above a coral line saying the year will be lost if she doesn’t. She does not know what Files is.

The app tells her she will lose everything, then gives instructions she cannot follow. That is the screen she puts the phone down on and waits for her son.

Her five
  1. Backup that never mentions iCloud — email it to herself or her son
  2. Send-to-one-person as the main road, posting as the alternative
  3. Fix the 0 tile, or say on that screen what it is
  4. Say each number once, and cut “plenty of room”
  5. Every time the app says save or share, say where it goes
“A number like twenty-five stops being a promise and turns into a debt the moment you show it to me as a grid of empty squares with a running score at the top. I cooked for Nancy every Tuesday for eleven years without counting once.”
“I have never in my life called what I do ‘acts of good’, and if my daughter-in-law saw that phrase on my phone with my name attached to it I would want the ground to open.”
What she loved: the ideas list, which she would read for pleasure. And “In honor of” — “my Don has been gone two years and there is not another app on this phone that has made room for him.”
Kayla Ocampo, 19
Runs a food drive off a group chat. Broke, permanently.
Would not use it

She went through all 89 ideas and found eleven she can do for free. The rest have a receipt attached — doughnuts, pastries, adoption fees, gift cards kept in the car (she has no car), a stepladder, a monthly standing gift.

So she runs out of things she can afford at act 11 of 25, and looks at fourteen empty tiles for the rest of the year.

“That’s not a nudge, that’s a bill.”

On the card: “It’s a plaque.” A retirement gift. And it is generated for her — the app makes the trophy, she supplies the act. Anonymous mode posts only the card, which she called exactly backwards: the numbered trophy with the evidence stripped out.

Her five
  1. A real free-only filter, with honest costs on every idea
  2. Kill “act 2 of 25” from the card, or let her turn it off
  3. Make the sign-up sheet the front door, not a paragraph inside a form
  4. A letter written for somebody who isn’t fifty
  5. Let the card be ugly — no script, no balloon, no gold
“The single best thing in here — a shareable list where people claim one of the things you need, nothing to sign up to — is buried in a form field. If you rebuilt the whole app around that one sentence I’d use it on Monday.”
Two of the ideas — fund a classroom wish list, clear a school’s lunch balances — she was on the receiving end of five years ago.
Desmond Iyer, 37
Blind. Screen reader. Went through it by ear and by keyboard.
Can log a year. Cannot plan one.

The spine works — setup, the letter, act 0, log it, write it up, the card, the caption. It breaks at the date, and the date is the entire planning half of the app.

What stops him
  1. The date picker has no controls in it. The days are not buttons. Selected is a colour; today is a border. The only date he can set is “Today”.
  2. Every date button says the wrong thing, permanently. It reads “pick a date” whether empty or set to next March — so he cannot read back a date already in his own journal.
  3. Eight back buttons with no name at all
  4. The month calendar is a wall of numbers — done, planned and today are three colours and no words
“Do one and two and I’ll use this. I mean that literally. Right now I could log it. I just couldn’t plan it.”
He went out of his way on what works: the 25 tiles are real buttons whose labels say their state in words. Screens announce themselves. Sheets handle focus properly. The dialogs are textbook. And the card is live text, not a picture — he can read his own card, and it should never be flattened into an image.
Ruth Kinnaird, 55
Checked 23 claims in the ideas library. Thirteen findings.
Content audit
The three that would embarrass somebody
  1. The organ donor / marrow line is wrong in every clause. The marrow registry is not a form — a swab kit is posted to you. It is age-limited, 18 to 40. And it is not once in a lifetime. It is tagged Today · An hour, so it is written to be acted on within the hour.
  2. “Registering voters is nonpartisan and legal everywhere.” The legal-everywhere half is the untrue half. Texas requires county deputisation to handle completed forms; Florida requires registering as an organisation, with real fines.
  3. “Tools, paint and lumber to a Habitat ReStore.” ReStores cannot accept paint. The reader loads the car before they find out.

And one she would not ship on principle: “Don’t overthink the allergies, the welcome is the point.” The only line in 89 that tells a reader to set aside a safety consideration.

Nineteen consistency findings. The worst: the You screen promises “Nothing leaves it” and forty words later tells you to put it in iCloud Drive. Three screens are all headed Plan & Log. And “Read Jessica’s letter again” names her, which your own rule says never travels into anybody’s copy.

Where she says the writing is genuinely good — and she is not generous by default: “Dignity travels in small denominations.” · “‘You are great’ evaporates, ‘the way you handled June’ does not.” · and on screen, “This is the journal entry. The post comes out of it, not the other way round” — which she called the clearest sentence in the product.
Priya Raghunathan, 43
What happens after the post goes up.
The social read
“The number creates demand for recipients, and nobody has asked what it is like to be the supply.”

By September, somebody who is behind does not look for the greatest need. They look for the nearest one — a friend, a neighbour, an older relative. Help that arrives in a cluster at the end of a quarter reads as administrative, and people can feel the difference.

The structural error: helpers and helped share one list. The People screen holds “everyone who’s been part of an act”, tagging draws from it, so the person you helped ends up beside the friend who came along, with the whole app pointed at getting their handle onto a post. They must not share a list. That one fix makes several others unnecessary.

“Ray took ten seconds to say yes to his bins going out. He did not say yes to being on page 114 of a book.”
Her five
  1. Recipients get their own field, and never enter People or tagging
  2. Anonymity becomes a property of the act, chosen at planning — and carries into the printed book
  3. Split the sign-up sheet: “I’m organising” and “this is for someone” behave nothing alike
  4. Count the asks. Warn at the third with no yes. Let a decline leave no trace
  5. Say once, on the home screen, that an empty tile is fine
Where she says publicness genuinely works: the acts where being seen is the good — a review, an email to a principal cc’ing the teacher. Act 0 as a commitment device. And the private send offered at equal weight on the posting screen, which she called a real piece of moral design.
Hollis Wray
The calendar. Six of eight floating dates compute correctly.
Calendar audit

Beyond the Wreaths date: Teacher Appreciation Week is in there twice — once correctly as a floating week, once wrongly pinned to a fixed 1 May with the same advice word for word. And Camp season is four months late: pinned to June, when fees and scholarship places close January to March.

Nothing religious is in the calendar at all. Each kind needs different machinery — Ramadan and Eid must be computed and shown as provisional; Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur can be exact but begin at sundown the evening before; Diwali cannot be ruled and needs a table; Easter is ten lines of arithmetic. He supplied 2026 and 2027 dates for all of them.

His most valuable point is not a date. The calendar fires on the day of the event, when the thing a person has to do — register, apply, sponsor, get cleared — happened weeks earlier. He rewrote the lead time for eighteen entries.

The single highest-value change: “Background checks take weeks” should fire twice a year, in January and July — because everything it unlocks has an autumn intake.

What I would do first

  1. The Wreaths date. Wrong, checked, and the only finding that puts somebody somewhere on the wrong day. One line.
  2. The date picker’s days become buttons. Unlocks the whole planning half of the app for anyone using a screen reader. Twenty minutes.
  3. The five date buttons stop lying about the date they hold. Three lines, fixes every date field at once.
  4. The three wrong claims in the ideas — marrow registry, voter registration, ReStore paint — and the allergies line.
  5. The home screen clock, which three of them stopped on separately.
