50 Acts of Good · panel 11 · the sign-up sheet

A hole with a lid on it

Seven people looked at the poster, the page behind the code, and the claim. All seven stopped at the same place — and it is not a place anyone has looked before.

Seven of seven — unanimous, a first for this panel

Nobody can hand a thing back.

Every one of them took something in their head, and every one of them then thought about the Saturday that goes wrong — a night shift, a fever, a mother in hospital. There is no way to give it back, so they don’t take anything at all.

That is not a claim lost. That is worse — that is a hole with a lid on it. Marcus, who would publish these by the dozen

The page says “In good hands.” The organiser believes it. Nobody turns up. Four of them said they would rather take nothing than risk being the hole, which means the damage is invisible — it looks exactly like a quiet sheet.

This is already written down as “still open” on the sheet, third in a list of three. The panel says it is not third. It is the whole credibility of the thing.

Six of seven

The poster outlives the act, and then it breaks.

A flyer stays on a wall for years. Scan an old one today and you get a blank browser error. Four of them said the same thing unprompted: they would assume they had done something wrong. Trent read it as a scam. Sherri: “My church has a flyer up from 2019. Somebody’s mother is going to scan that.”

The front door was deliberately made warm rather than a tidy error. The sheet that has finished never was.

What I need you to rule — six things

Ruling 1 · all seven

Giving a thing back

Someone takes the pans, then can’t. Today their only option is to go quiet. Quiet is worse than no.

1AThe page remembers, on that phone, that the row is theirs — and offers “I can’t after all” right up to the day. The row simply opens again. No name, no explanation, nobody told off. my recommendation
1BThe same, but the row comes back to the top of your list marked came back, so you see it rather than discovering a gap.
1CLeave it. They text you — with a number they were never given.

Ruling 2 · six of seven

The poster that outlived its act

A code that leads nowhere is the normal ending for a piece of paper, not an edge case.

2AA finished sheet keeps its page and says so kindly — this one is done, thank you for stopping — ending on the creed, the same as when everything is taken.
2BThe same, and it says what the act became: “Eight firefighters got dinner. Thank you for scanning.” The stranger who was thirty seconds too late still gets something. my recommendation
2CLeave the blank error page.

Ruling 3 · three of seven, and act 22 agrees

How many are still wanted

Marcus asks for eight casseroles and nineteen arrive. Brianne runs every sheet in numbers. One line, one person is a shopping list, not a sign-up sheet.

3AA row can want more than one: six dozen cookies — two dozen still wanted. my recommendation
3BA row can simply be taken by several people, with no number at all — “a few of these”.
3CLeave it at one each.

Ruling 4 · four of seven

What you are promising, before you promise it

They all stopped on the same line: “Someone with a van, Saturday morning.” Which Saturday. How far. Brianne’s husband works nights; Trent does shifts. Nobody asked for the address — they asked whether they can keep the promise.

4AThe date and the rough area, and nothing more — Saturday the 14th, east side of town. The street still only arrives in a private message. my recommendation
4BThe date only. No area.
4CLeave it — they find out after they have said yes.
Fair warning: this brushes a ruling you already closed — the page never shows where and when. That ruling was written to stop a church wall broadcasting a drop-off address, and 4A does not do that. But it is your closed ruling, so it is yours to reopen or leave shut.

Ruling 5 · Paula, on the printing

The poster looks finished

She read it as an order of service. “Finished things don’t get scanned.” On a board beside a handwritten flyer with tear-off tabs, ours reads as the official one that has already been dealt with — and the 28-door flyer that actually worked was made at a kitchen table. Two separate notes, both yours because both are looks:

5AThe two lines at the top stop competing — at the moment the coral line and the black line are the same size with an even rule between them, so neither one is the headline. One of them gets smaller.
5BThe thin gold frame comes off the printed sheet only, and stays everywhere else. A home printer pulls paper a millimetre or two crooked and a crooked frame is the one thing everybody sees.
5CLeave the poster exactly as it is.

Ruling 6 · Renata and Sherri, from opposite directions

The example on the poster

Renata: every one of those four things costs money — bedding, pans, staples, a van. She has four dollars a week and two hands, and the poster asked for neither. Sherri, separately: a family arriving in March is the one example half her nine thousand women would read as a political position rather than a kindness.

6AChange the example to act 22’s own shape — a meal for the fire station. A pan of something hot. A gallon of sweet tea. An hour on Saturday to carry boxes. No politics, and not everything costs. my recommendation
6BBigger than the example: the app always nudges you to include at least one row that costs nothing — an hour, a lift, a pair of hands — so the sheet is never a wall of receipts.
6CLeave the example alone.
Sherri also named the front page: “put a little more good into the world” reads to her as one side of her town. She praised “a neighbor, a congregation or a staff room” in the same breath — so the voice can do it. Named, not built; say the word and I’ll draw options.

The sentence to read twice

This page is the only part of it built for someone with nobody. No account, no tab of faces, no who’s in it with you. You’ve accidentally built the version that works for me and put it on the outside of the building. Dana — two friends, and the app’s sharpest blind spot
