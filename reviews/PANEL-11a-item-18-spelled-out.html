50 Acts of Good · the sign-up sheet · item 18

The pans that never came

All seven testers stopped here, which has not happened before. It is one missing button, and it decides whether anybody trusts the sheet at all.

The problem, as it actually happens

Tuesday

She takes the pans.

She walks past the board at church, scans the code, and takes one thing. It costs her nothing to say yes — that is the point of the whole design, and it works.

She gives her first name and her phone number. The row closes. She feels good about it.

Thursday

Her mother goes into hospital.

She opens the page again. There is nothing on it that is hers any more — her row just says In good hands, the same as everyone else’s. There is no button. There is no name to call. She was never given one; the organiser messages her.

So she does the only thing left. She says nothing, and hopes to manage it anyway.

Saturday

There are no pans.

Your list says every row is taken. It has said so all week. You have not chased anybody, because there was nothing to chase.

The family opens a box with a hole in it, and the hole had a name on it the whole time — you just could not see it, and neither could she.

In good handsRight up to the morning it wasn’t.

And this is the half you would never find out about

Most people never take anything in the first place.

Four of the seven said it outright: knowing they could not back out, they would rather take nothing at all than risk being the hole. They did not think of that as refusing. They just closed the page.

I would rather take nothing than be the hole. Renata — two jobs, three children

So the sheet fills slowly, and you conclude that people are busy, or that the act was not compelling, or that the poster did not work. A sheet nobody dares claim from looks exactly like a sheet nobody cared about. There is no way to tell the two apart from where you are standing.

Why it is worse than losing a claim

A claim that gets lost

The row goes back to saying Take this. Somebody else takes it. You never even know it happened.

Annoying. Self-correcting.

A claim nobody can return

The row keeps saying In good hands. Nobody else can take it, because as far as the page is concerned it is handled.

It does not correct itself. It gets quieter and quieter, right up to the morning, and then it is too late to fix.

The solution, drawn

The fix

The page remembers whose row it is.

Nothing changes for anybody else. But on her phone — the one she scanned with — her row still looks like hers, and underneath it there is one line she can tap right up to the day:

“I can’t after all.”

The row opens again. Nobody is named, nobody explains themselves, nobody is told off. The next person who scans the code sees a thing that needs doing, which is exactly what they came for.

It needs no account and no message sent — the page simply knows, on that phone, which row was taken from it. That is the whole of it.

The real gain is the part you will never see.

Putting a way out on the page is what makes saying yes cheap again. People take things when taking them is not a trap — the button’s job is mostly to be there, not to be pressed.

Your three choices

18A

The way out, quietly  my recommendation

Exactly what is drawn above. The row reopens; your list simply shows it as needing doing again, the same as if nobody had ever taken it.

Costs you nothing to look at. You find out by the row being open, which is the thing you were going to check anyway.

18B

The way out, and you are told

The same, but the row comes back to the top of your list, marked came back, so you see that something changed rather than discovering a gap in a long list.

Better if you run big sheets and do not re-read them. Slightly heavier — it puts a small piece of bookkeeping on a page that currently has none.

18C

Leave it as it is

Nobody can hand anything back. The people who would have are the ones who take nothing.

The honest version of this choice: the sheet works best for people whose week never goes wrong.

Say 18A, 18B or 18C. Nothing is built.

The other five — 19 through 23 — are still where they were, in the panel review and in your open-questions file. This one was worth spelling out on its own because it is the only finding all seven agreed on, and because it is the cheapest of the six to build.
