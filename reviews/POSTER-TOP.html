The top of the poster

The honest answer

F is right, and it fixes something neither of us had named.

I expected narrowing the gap between the two lines from a quarter to about a twelfth to undo the hierarchy. It doesn’t — shrunk down, the ask still arrives first, because size was never the only thing carrying it. Coral against black, centred, wider letter-spacing, and first on the page all still point the same way.

And there was a real fault underneath your instinct. In A and B the black title sits at the same size as it always did — smaller than the paragraph directly beneath it. A title smaller than its own body copy is backwards, and it is part of why the top of this sheet reads as flat no matter what the coral line does. Fifteen per cent lifts it clear of that without letting it challenge the ask.

I drew 15% on the ask first and it did almost nothing. You were right to push that too.

At fifteen, the four tops were near enough indistinguishable in the strip above — a pixel and a half at that size, swallowed by distance. Worse, what was arriving first in every one of them was the colour, not the size: coral against black does the work at ten feet, and a small size step underneath it is noise.

Twenty-five is past that threshold. The ask is now unmistakably the headline both up close and shrunk down, and the poster still reads as composed rather than shouty. Your number, and it is the right one.

And 25 quietly settles the thing I was going to argue about.

At fifteen I was going to push you to C, because the size step was too weak to carry the hierarchy on its own and the gold rule — equal air above and below — was reading as a fence between two equal things.

At twenty-five the size does carry it, so the even rule stops mattering. C is now a refinement, not a fix. Take it if you like the tighter grouping; leave it and nothing is wrong.

Why I would still not go to D, even though it fixes Paula’s exact complaint.

She said you cannot tell what the poster is about. D fixes that literally. But the line that made Trent stop reading was “WE COULD USE A HAND” — “that’s how a man asks for help” — and Marcus knew what the sheet was before he read a word, because the shape said it.

A poster on a wall has one job first: stop somebody. Explaining comes second, and the reason is still right there, a line below. D wins the argument and loses the wall.

Ruled: F — the ask at 25%, the black title at 15%. Written into the poster spec.

Nothing is printed yet — the poster is generated by the app side of the sheet, which isn’t built. This settles it so it is right the first time it comes off a printer. 22B (the gold frame off the printed sheet) is still open, and so is C if you ever want the rule tucked up.
