Why this matters more than crookedness

Almost no home printer can print to the edge of the paper.

Most inkjets and nearly every laser printer hold back a strip around all four sides — usually around a quarter of an inch, sometimes more at the bottom where the rollers grip. Anything inside that strip is simply not printed.

The frame is currently specified about a quarter of an inch in from the paper edge, which is exactly on that line. It will print beautifully on some machines and lose part of a side on others — and because it is a continuous rectangle, losing part of one side is far uglier than losing part of a margin. A frame with a gap in it looks broken, not close.

Moving it in fixes the crooked problem too, for free.

Nobody can see that a frame is crooked on its own. They see it by comparing it to the edge of the paper next to it. The closer those two lines are, the louder the mismatch — a millimetre of feed skew is obvious at a quarter-inch gap and invisible at half an inch.

So one change answers both: the frame survives every printer, and the small skew every home printer has stops being visible.

What it costs, honestly.

The text block gets narrower — about a fifteenth of the width at option 3. The list of needs wraps a little sooner on a long line, and the whole sheet sits in more white.

That is not only a cost. Paula’s note was that this poster reads as finished and official, and a wide quiet border is the most formal thing on a page — so option 3 pulls slightly in the direction she warned about. Option 2 is the compromise if that worries you more than a broken frame does.

Something else, now that it is on a real sheet of paper

The poster fills about two-thirds of the page and the frame encloses the rest.

Look at any of the three above. The bottom third is empty, inside the gold line. That is not a margin choice — it is because this was designed as a card that flows to whatever height it needs, and it has never once been laid out on the paper it prints on.

A frame around a lot of nothing reads as a page that ran out, which is the opposite of the “finished and official” problem — it looks unfinished. Three ways out: the content sits centred in the frame rather than at the top; the type grows to fill an A4 sheet properly; or the frame stops where the content does and the sheet is trimmed or folded.

Named, not built. It is a separate decision from the margin and a bigger one, and it wants its own look. Say the word when you want it drawn.
